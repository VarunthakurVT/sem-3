Less-than type frequency curve

More-than type frequency curve

Corresponding frequencies were 5, 8, 15, 12, 7, and 3, giving a total frequency of 50

## Constructing the Curves and Reading the Median

Graph axes used class-boundary values 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 on the x-axis

Cumulative frequency was placed on the y-axis up to a maximum of 50, with marked levels such as 10, 20, 30, and 40

Less-than points were plotted as:

(0, 0), (5, 5), (10, 13), (15, 28), (20, 40), (25, 47), and (30, 50)

More-than points were plotted as:

(0, 50), (5, 45), (10, 37), (15, 22), (20, 10), (25, 3), and (30, 0)

Joining the less-than points creates the less-than ogive; joining the more-than points creates the more-than ogive

The graphical median is found where the two curves intersect

A perpendicular from their intersection to the x-axis gives the median value

Median divides the dataset into two equal parts

The instructor initially used a hypothetical intersection between 15 and 20 and an illustrative value of 70, then clarified that the rough graph’s intersection appeared between 10 and 15

## Average as a Measure of Central Tendency

Average is a single value within the range of the data that represents the values in the distribution

The average lies between the maximum and minimum values and represents the central position of the data

Average is therefore considered a measure of central tendency

A good average should be:

Simple to understand

Simple to compute

Based on the observations

Based on all observations

Properly defined

Stable across different samples

Suitable for further mathematical treatment

Sampling stability means that different samples should produce approximately the same average value

Averages from two or more series should allow calculation of a combined average

The main objectives of an average are:

Representing an entire dataset with one value

Facilitating comparison between two or more series

Measures of average listed in the lecture included mean, median, mode, “metric mean,” and harmonic mean

## Mean for Individual or Ungrouped Data

Three series types were identified:

Ungrouped or individual series

Discrete frequency distribution

Continuous frequency distribution

For ungrouped data with observations (X_1, X_2, X_3, \ldots, X_n), the mean is the sum of observations divided by the number of observations

Direct method formula: (\bar X = \frac{\sum X}{N})

Example observations were 8, 12, 20, 14, and 16

Their total was 70, and the number of observations was 5

Mean by the direct method: (70 \div 5 = 14)

---

## Shortcut Methods: Change of Origin and Change of Scale

Change of origin subtracts or adds a common assumed value from every observation

With (D = X - A), the mean is calculated as:

(\bar X = A + \frac{\sum D}{N})

In the example, 12 was selected as the assumed value:

Deviations from 12 were −4, 0, 8, 2, and 4

Their sum was 10

Mean: (12 + \frac{10}{5} = 14)

Change of scale divides or multiplies every deviation by a common value to simplify larger calculations

When both origin and scale are changed, the common divisor was 2, and the transformed deviations summed to 5

The instructor emphasized that all three methods should give the same mean when the assumed value, deviations, and divisor are used consistently

Student questions focused on selecting the assumed value, using a central value to simplify calculations, and applying the formula correctly

A small practice dataset—2, 4, 6, 8, and 12—was given for calculation by all three methods
