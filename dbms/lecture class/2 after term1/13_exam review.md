## Writing Complete, To-the-Point Answers

**Primary key vs. foreign key**: A definition alone was considered insufficient for a five-mark question.

Define each key and explain its relationship to the candidate-key set; the teacher emphasized identifying the relevant subset.

Add an example and, where the marks require it, SQL syntax or code.

Extra writing does not earn marks if it misses the requested points; answers should be complete but focused.

**ER diagrams**: Define the diagram and use it to communicate the relevant components.

Include applicable concepts such as multivalued and composite attributes, primary and foreign keys, weak and strong entities, relationships, aggregation, and specialization.

Clear diagrams can make explanations easier and reduce unnecessary prose.

**Three-level DBMS architecture**: The expected levels were external, conceptual, and internal.

The teacher cautioned against confusing DBMS architecture with one-tier, two-tier, and three-tier architecture, or with levels of abstraction.

The architecture question also called for an example and a diagram.

## Matching Answers to the Exact Database Question

**Database users**: Relevant roles included database administrator, developer, simple user, and designer; responses should name the number or types requested.

**Relational model**: The answer should explain that data is represented in tables rather than drifting into an unrelated topic.

**Database vs. DBMS**: The question asked for a database, not specifically a DBMS.

Explain the idea of organized, related data and its storage; do not treat Oracle, MySQL, or PostgreSQL as databases themselves when referring to them as software.

A relational-database answer was acceptable in the course context, while non-relational examples could also fit a general database question.

**SQL commands and DML**: The teacher distinguished a broad question about SQL command types from a separate question asking for the role of DDL with an example.

DML examples identified included `INSERT`, `UPDATE`, and `DELETE`; `CREATE` belongs to DDL.

CRUD can be explained when the question asks for SQL command types, but it does not replace a specific explanation of DML or DDL.

`GRANT` and `REVOKE` were described separately; `COMMIT` and `ROLLBACK` were also mentioned in discussing command categories.

Reuse an explanation when the questions overlap, rather than repeating the same material without purpose.

## Planning Time and Presenting Answers Clearly

Spend a couple of minutes reading the full question paper before starting and identify easy, difficult, and high-value questions.

Set a time limit for each answer; one example was limiting a five-mark ER-diagram answer to about 10 minutes, then adding detail only if time remains.

Use diagrams wherever they clarify the answer, especially for ER diagrams and DBMS architecture.

The teacher noted that the exam was designed around an earlier schedule that anticipated multiple exam sessions across three days; the schedule changed, and marks were adjusted. The teacher said some initial one-mark questions were later changed to 2.5 marks each.

## Marking, Academic Integrity, and AI Use

Marks depend on relevant, accurate content rather than answer length; the teacher said marks could increase or decrease after cross-checking.

The teacher acknowledged that the available exam time was limited and said a more thorough attempt would have required about an hour and a half.

Students were warned against using phones or submitting generic AI-generated answers. Similar prompts can produce similar responses, while answers in students’ own natural wording are more distinct.

AI tools can help generate a detailed prompt, but students still need to understand the subject and write the answer themselves.

English spelling, grammar, and readability matter for writing answers and for practical technical work, including finding Python code or prompting for machine-learning topics such as linear and logistic regression.
