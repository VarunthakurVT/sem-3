## ER Diagram Symbols and Relationship Representation

Entities are represented using rectangles in an ER diagram

Relationships between entities are represented using diamonds

Attributes are represented using ovals

A diamond in an ER diagram represents a relationship condition, not a programming comparison or simple yes/no decision

Student–teacher examples were used to show relationships such as a teacher teaching students

A student can be taught by a teacher, and the relationship may be one-to-many, many-to-one, or many-to-many depending on the chosen scope

A single teacher may teach many students

Many teachers may teach many students when considering a complete semester or program

A teacher may teach multiple courses, while a course may be taken by multiple students

Relationship cardinality changes according to whether the model concerns one teacher, one course, one class, a semester, or the entire university

## Primary Keys and Attribute Types

A roll number was used as an example of a primary key

Primary keys must be unique

Primary keys cannot be null

Every student is expected to have a roll number, so the value is compulsory

In an ER diagram, a primary-key attribute is shown as an oval with an underline

**Composite attributes**: First name, middle name, and last name can be combined to represent a person’s complete name

Address can similarly be broken into multiple components, such as primary and secondary address details

Middle name is generally optional, whereas first name and last name are commonly compulsory in forms

**Multivalued attributes**: Phone number may contain multiple values because a person can have an alternate or additional phone number

A phone number is not necessarily compulsory; the required or optional status depends on the application’s requirements

Parent information may include parent name, parent phone number, mother’s number, and guardian information

Parent information can either be modeled as attributes of the student or separated into another entity when the requirements justify it

## Strong Entities, Weak Entities, and Derived Attributes

A strong entity has its own independent identity and properties, such as an account, education record, student, teacher, or course

A weak entity depends on another entity and is derived from it

Student-parent information was given as an example of a possible weak entity because it depends on the student entity

The instructor distinguished weak entities from weak attributes

**Derived attributes**: Age can be calculated from date of birth and therefore does not always need to be stored directly

Percentage can be derived from marks, such as marks obtained out of 100

Automatically calculated or auto-filled values may be generated by the system

Roll numbers can be automatically assigned sequentially through auto-increment

Name and date of birth may not uniquely identify a person because two people can share the same name and date of birth

A stronger unique identifier, such as an identification number, may be needed when uniqueness is required

Attributes that are directly stored are treated differently from values derived through calculations

## Connecting Tables Through Relationships

Tables do not connect merely because they contain similar names; relationships require a meaningful basis such as identifiers or defined associations

Teacher, student, course, and subject entities may all participate in related relationships

A timetable can act as an associative structure connecting multiple entities, including course, infrastructure, teacher, and student

Student registration primarily creates a relationship between the student and the course

The registered course may separately be associated with the teacher who teaches it

A university database may require many separate tables rather than placing all student, teacher, course, and examination data into one table

ER diagrams provide a visual sketch of the required data structure before implementation

Diagram elements discussed included rectangles, diamonds, ovals, arrows, dashed lines, parallelograms, and cylinders, depending on the type of representation

The diagram is a model; the detailed schema is defined later by specifying data types, sizes, constraints, and other implementation rules

## Schema Design, Constraints, and Normalization

A schema represents the structure of a table, including attribute names, data types, field sizes, and constraints

An ER diagram is not yet the final schema

The progression described was: requirements → ER diagram → schema → data modeling and implementation

Database design must begin with what users require and what information the application needs to display or store

Constraints impose restrictions such as uniqueness, required values, and valid data conditions

A very large table containing all student, teacher, course, and related information would become difficult to maintain

Repeatedly reading a table containing 90,000 or 100,000 rows can increase response time

Larger tables can reduce performance and increase hardware costs

Normalization divides an oversized table into smaller, logically organized tables

Denormalization may sometimes be considered for performance or practical requirements

Normal forms and normalization will be studied in greater detail after keys and relationship concepts

One-to-one, one-to-many, and many-to-one relationships were identified as upcoming or continuing topics
