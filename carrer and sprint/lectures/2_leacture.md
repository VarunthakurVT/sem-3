## Percentage and Defect-Free-Unit Calculations

Question 13 involved average values and percentage conversion, with intermediate figures including 2600, division by five, cancellation, and a reported value of 23.93

The instructor confirmed 94% as the correct answer for Question 13

Question 14 specifically asked for “defect free” units in Factory C in the year 2000

The instructor repeatedly distinguished defect-free units from defective units before proceeding with the calculation

The method used the complement of the defective percentage, such as 100% minus 14% or 100% minus 8%

Students produced conflicting answers, including 60 and 160, while other calculations referenced values such as 42, 5.4, 3.5, and 10.8

Careful use of plus and minus operations was highlighted; the final confirmed answer was 260

The instructor characterized the calculations as basic and checked whether everyone understood the method

## Student-Appearance and Qualification Tables

A separate table-based exercise compared the number of students appearing and qualifying across different subjects and years

Students were advised to identify trends and relationships instead of being intimidated by the size of the table

A repeated 25% relationship was used in the questions

One prompt asked for the number of qualified students in 1983 when qualification equaled 25% of the students who appeared in the same subject

Geography was referenced as an example where the same calculation pattern applied

A mathematics comparison involving 1981 and “95” was interpreted as a 20% difference

A calculation using 40 and 160 showed that 40 is one-fourth of 160, producing 25%

A history-related question used the value 84, with 1985 identified as the answer

The instructor encouraged students to copy or retain the question before moving to the next exercise

## Refinery-to-Distributor Minimum-Cost Problem

The final exercise used a large cost table representing movement from refinery to depot and depot to distributor

The question asked for the least cost of sending one unit from any refinery to any distributor

The route was not treated as a simple direct connection; the product moved from refinery to depot and then from depot to distributor

Students were told to inspect the options for zero-cost entries first rather than calculating every possible route

Multiple zero entries were identified, including routes involving labels such as A, B/C, D/C, and E; some labels were unclear because of the recording quality

Since a valid two-stage route had cost 0, the minimum possible cost was confirmed as 0

If no zero-cost route existed, the instructor indicated that students would need to compare the next-lowest refinery-to-depot and depot-to-distributor combinations

The instructor warned students not to abandon a question merely because the table looked large and emphasized careful route interpretation before calculating

The session ended as the class prepared to move to the next instructor’s session
